0. Abstract

Jetsam (ticker JTM) is a proof-of-work Layer 1 whose every block carries a recursive proof. Each proof covers the history from the root of its relation generation; below that root, the chain rests on checkpoints published in the binary (section 4.2). A joining node authenticates the current state, verifies one proof, checks the last eight blocks and the proof of work of the headers. It never replays transactions, so the cost of checking proofs does not grow with the age of the chain; checking headers does grow, linearly, but lightly.

The public network has been producing blocks since 3 September 2026 [chain] and has crossed five consensus upgrades, the latest at block 38 400 on 13 October 2026 [chain]. The chain has stopped twice: for 6 146 s on 7 September 2026, for a reason unrelated to an upgrade, and for about 49 minutes on 13 October 2026, in the hours after the fifth upgrade, when most of the hashrate stayed on the old rules (section 2). Supply is hard-capped at 21 000 000 JTM [code]. The genesis block carries no coinbase and no coins existed before mining [code]; a development allocation of 10 % of the block subsidy, paid by consensus on a published schedule that ends, runs for the first two target-time years (section 7.3). We state both facts together, because "no premine" alone would be the flattering half.

Building that machinery gives Jetsam something most chains have to buy: recursive proving capacity, a verifier already inside consensus, and a settlement layer. The product Jetsam is being built towards is the sale of that capacity — a registry where an application can prove, in public and permanently, that it followed a rule it said it would follow. Getting there required freeing the GPUs that used to mine, which is why the proof of work has been moved onto a memory-latency-bound function on which a server CPU socket does work of the same order as a datacentre accelerator, for an estimated three to five times less power [estimate — extrapolated from a single measured core; see section 5.4]. That move happened at block 24 846. The next step, the client proof slot, opened at block 38 400 with an empty catalogue: the machinery shipped before the first customer, and this document says so rather than implying a market exists.

Nothing in the consensus rules depends on an elliptic curve. Spending is a proof of a hash preimage, not a signature.

1. Why Jetsam exists

1.1 History is a tax that compounds

On a conventional chain, the price of being able to disbelieve everyone is that you must replay everything. A new node downloads every block ever mined and re-executes it before it can say one true thing about the present. That cost rises forever. In practice it does not stop people from running nodes — it stops them from running new ones, and the set of parties who can independently check the chain quietly narrows to the ones who started early or bought a server.

Jetsam's answer is the one thing recursive proofs are genuinely good at. Each block ships a proof of exactly one statement: the previous proof was valid. Induction does the rest — back to the root of the block's relation generation, not further. When the rules change the constraint system, a new generation starts from a new root, and the history below that root is held by checkpoints published in the binary rather than by the proof (section 4.2). A node that verifies the newest proof has verified the chain down to that root, without replaying any transaction. At height 3 000 000 the proof check costs what it costs at height 10. The headers are another matter: they are kept permanently and a fresh node checks the proof of work of each one, which grows linearly with height — about 1.85 ms per header on one server core [measured, stated in the source], so on the order of an hour and a half of one core at height 3 000 000 [derived].

The state follows the same rule. Coins live in a fixed accumulator of slots; spending an output erases its slot in place and the next transaction reuses the seat. There is no append-only set of spent outputs growing forever. State is proportional to live coins, not to everything that ever happened.

1.2 Proving is a cost centre almost everywhere

Every chain that has adopted zero-knowledge proofs has had to answer the same question: who pays for the proving hardware? The answers are always one of three — subsidise it with inflation, pass it to users as fees, or concentrate it into the handful of operators who can afford the machines.

Jetsam starts from the opposite premise. The proving capacity a chain builds for itself is an asset it can sell. A network that already runs a recursive proof system for its own blocks has, in place and paid for, the three things a proof market needs: hardware, a verifier that is already in consensus, and settlement. The marginal cost of proving somebody else's computation is the slot in the schedule it occupies, not the capital.

1.3 The customer is not another blockchain

The market of blockchains that need proofs is small and crowded with well-funded competitors, all of them fighting over rollups. The market of applications that have to prove they do what they claim is not small, and it is not served by the systems fighting over rollups.

That market is growing for reasons that have nothing to do with cryptography. Software agents now take actions with money, data and third-party systems; the people who deploy them are being asked, by customers and by regulators, to show what the agent did and did not do. The usual answer is a log file and a promise — a log file the party under scrutiny wrote about itself.

The value of a registry has never come from its technology. It comes from the fact that somebody requires you to be in it. TLS certificates and compliance attestations are the model: nobody buys the cryptography, they buy the badge — and the badge is worth something because a counterparty asks to see it. Jetsam's long-term bet is that "verified on JTM" can be such a badge, and that a chain which already verifies proofs inside its own consensus is the cheapest possible place to hold that registry.

1.4 What a proof guarantees — and what it does not

This is the sentence most projects in this field leave out, so it goes early.

A proof guarantees that a computation was performed correctly. It says nothing about whether the inputs were true. A service can prove, impeccably, that it applied the correct rule to data it invented. For a proof to be worth anything to a third party, the input has to be committed by somebody other than the party being checked.

That single constraint decides which uses hold up and which are marketing. It is why section 6 proposes the uses it proposes: cases where the inputs are naturally witnessed by others — signed API calls, transactions, writes into somebody else's system.

A second limit follows from the first. We never prove the quality of a judgement. Not that an agent reasoned well, not that an answer was correct, not that a model is good. We prove compliance with rules that were defined in advance and are checkable by a machine. The failure mode that kills projects in this field is selling a vague guarantee and delivering a narrow one. We would rather state the narrow one first.

3. Design principles

Five choices explain most of the rest of this document. Each is stated with the reason, because a principle without a reason is a slogan.

3.1 No curves in consensus — there is nothing for Shor to break

Jetsam has no signature scheme. An address is a Poseidon2b digest of a 256-bit secret, and spending is a proof of knowledge of that preimage, bound to the transaction body and folded into the block's recursive proof [code]. The spending proof is witness-hiding by construction: it is built so as not to reveal the secret, and the source certifies the finite-field lemmas this relies on — but no complete hiding proof has been written, so we do not call it zero-knowledge. There are no pairings and no elliptic curves in the consensus rules, in the proof system, or in the proof of work.

That is a claim somebody will try to break, so here is exactly where the boundary runs. No elliptic-curve or signature library is reachable from any consensus, proof-system or proof-of-work crate. Curves do appear in the dependency graph, and it would be dishonest to imply otherwise: the peer-to-peer layer brings in an Ed25519 peer identity and an X25519 Noise handshake, with the usual supporting crates. Every one of them traces back to the networking library. None can authorise a transaction, create value, or satisfy any ownership rule in consensus — they identify and encrypt a link between two machines.

The consequence, stated narrowly. There is no elliptic-curve scheme in this chain, so there is nothing here for Shor's algorithm to attack and nothing to migrate when large quantum computers arrive. Grover's algorithm still applies to preimage search and to the proof system, here as everywhere; it is a square-root speed-up. The only quantum figure the source certifies is modest: about 64.7 bits for the production profile in an idealised, sequential quantum-random-oracle model [code] — see the table below.

The consequence, stated honestly. Removing signatures does not remove the question, it moves it: security now rests on Poseidon2b and on the soundness of the proof system. That is a real dependency, and here is where it actually stands. The production profile targets 128-bit soundness, and two different statements sit behind that target.

We take the lowest of these figures, about 64 bits provable, as the floor we are prepared to defend, and we do not average it upwards. Exactly which component of the stack each figure governs is a point we are still documenting; until that is published, read the 64-bit figure as the one that applies. Only the conjectured figures reach 128 bits. The capacity conjecture is relied on by every deployed FRI system we know of and it remains a conjecture; for the compact profile, lifting the provable figure to 128 bits by query count alone would take roughly 148 queries and about double the opening size, which is a trade we have not made.

We keep proven, inherited and conjectured apart deliberately and everywhere in this document. Merging them is the single most common way a project in this field is taken apart, and averaging them into one confident number would buy a better sentence at the cost of the document.

What "signatureless" does not mean: it does not mean private. Addresses, amounts and fees are public (section 4.4).

3.2 The state forgets, and the proofs make forgetting safe

An append-only ledger is a promise to store everything forever. Jetsam erases spent slots and reuses them. What makes that safe is that the proof, not the archive, is what carries the guarantee that the erased history was valid — back to the root of its relation generation, with the checkpoints of section 4.2 below it. Net-new state growth is priced by a consensus burn, so the party that makes every node carry more state is the party that pays for it (section 7.4).

3.3 The proof of work lives outside the circuit

The nonce enters neither the constraint system nor the HistoryStep relation [code]. This is a deliberate architectural choice with a concrete consequence: the proof-of-work function can be replaced without regenerating a single matrix. On a chain that proves its own proof of work, changing the mining function is a multi-month relation migration. Here it is a consensus parameter and a new digest function.

That property is not an elegance. It is the only real mitigation Jetsam has against a hardware monoculture forming around its mining function (section 9.1).

3.4 Relations have generations, and a node that cannot honour one refuses to start

When the rules change in a way that changes the constraint system, the matrices change with them, and a block proved against the old matrices is only ever verifiable against the old matrices. Jetsam makes this explicit: a binary carries every relation generation it needs and selects by the block's own height, never by the tip. A node whose activation height is armed but which carries no matching parameter pack refuses to start, rather than running happily until it reaches the fork and then stopping the chain for everybody [code].

Generations are deliberately rare. After five consensus upgrades on the public network there are exactly three of them [code]: the third, v1.5, moved the two proof classes from circuit sizes 2^22 and 2^24 to 2^23 and 2^25 rows, because the client arm is carried inside the relation. Consensus changes that do not touch the constraint system — a wire-format change, or a new proof-of-work digest — are not generations: the chain crosses them on the parameters it already has. The proof-of-work replacement of section 5 added none, which is section 3.3 paying for itself. A reader will meet the figure "m = 25" in the release notes: it is the size of the large circuit, not a count of anything per block.

3.5 No general-purpose virtual machine

Jetsam will not ship an EVM or a WASM VM inside its circuit, and this is a permanent choice rather than a missing feature. Every opcode of a general VM becomes constraint rows, and the budget does not survive the comparison. The row budget of a block would stretch to a few thousand RISC-V cycles; a single token transfer on such a VM takes tens of thousands [estimate]. The gap is one to two orders of magnitude, before any application logic runs at all.

The consequence is section 6.3: clients get a catalogue of parameterised computations, not a programming language. That is a smaller promise, and it is one the circuit can actually keep.

4. Architecture

4.1 One permutation: Poseidon2b over GF(2^128)

Every hash in Jetsam — Merkle trees, transaction roots, state commitments, the proof-of-work seed — is built from a single permutation, Poseidon2b, over the binary tower field GF(2^128). We did not invent it: it is published work (Grassi et al., ePrint 2025/1893) and it is named in our references and in the repository's NOTICE.

A binary field is chosen over the prime fields common in ZK systems for one practical reason. On a binary field the arithmetic is XOR and carry-less multiplication rather than modular reduction — the same algebra the proof system natively speaks, and an operation that maps directly onto the PCLMULQDQ instruction, present on mainstream x86 CPUs since 2010–2011, and onto PMULL on ARM. The hash and the circuit are not two worlds that have to be bridged.

Domain separation is explicit rather than implied: each use of the permutation absorbs a distinct 8-byte tag, so a digest computed for one purpose can never be replayed as a digest for another [code].

Carry-less multiplication is the one hard hardware requirement in the whole system. A node needs SSE4.1 and PCLMULQDQ on x86-64, or NEON and PMULL on ARM64, and refuses to start otherwise rather than silently falling back to a path that would make it useless [code].

4.2 HistoryStep: the block that proves the block before it

Each block carries a proof of one relation, HistoryStep, stating that the block's state transition follows the consensus rules and that the previous block's terminal commitment was valid. The proof for block n never re-opens history — the proof for block n−1 already vouched for it.

How far back the induction reaches. Not to genesis. Each relation generation (section 3.4) roots its recursion at the block just below its activation height, and a proof at a given height covers the blocks above that root and nothing at or below it [code]. Each proof therefore covers the history from the root of its generation; below that, the chain rests on the permanent headers, whose proof of work every node checks, and on checkpoints published in the binary — today the hashes of blocks 24 846, 38 399 and 38 400 [code]. Under the v1.4.3 rules a proof covered blocks 17 750 onward; since block 38 400 it covers 38 400 onward, and v1.5.1 pins 38 399, the last block both rule sets share, and 38 400, the activation block itself (section 8).

The proving stack is assembled from published components:

component what it is
constraint system R1CS over GF(2), block-diagonal, with its witness generation
polynomial commitment multilinear, over binary fields, in the Binius line — GF(2) packing, additive NTT, Merkle commitment, ring-switching and a BaseFold-style folding

The workspace also carries an executable soundness certificate: it loads the real parameters from the crates that define them, returns an error and fails its tests if any of them drifts from the profile it is supposed to be [code]. It is a test-time check, not a build-time one, and it currently loads the launch-generation proof classes (2^22 and 2^24 rows) only: the v1.5 classes (2^23 and 2^25) are not yet put through it [code].

4.3 State: a slot accumulator, not a growing set

Coins live in a fixed-size accumulator addressed by slot. Spending erases a slot and returns it to the pool; the next output takes the seat. The accumulator expands only when occupancy crosses a threshold, and the expansion is a protocol event, not a per-transaction cost.

The practical figure is in section 2: the whole live state of the public network encodes to about 1.5 MB at today's slot count [derived from the v2.0 measurement of 747 KB for 15 306 slots; not re-measured]. That is the number behind the claim that anyone can audit the live state from a browser tab.

What "finality depth" means here, exactly. It is a rule each node applies to itself: a node will not reorganise more than 8 blocks below its own tip [code]. It is not a guarantee that the network cannot fork. If two branches ever diverge by more than 8 blocks, the nodes on each side refuse the other side permanently, and the split is resolved by operators, not by the protocol. A node that does not hold the prefix — a fresh sync, for instance — chooses by work alone, and the depth rule does not protect it; the source says so itself [code]. On a young network with few miners (section 9.8) a transaction should be treated as settled when it is 8 blocks deep and the network has not reported a split, not before.

Anchoring on the parent rather than on the block's own timestamp removes a grinding vector: dating a block earlier can no longer make it cheaper. It also means ASERT absorbs a loss of hashrate gracefully and does not absorb a stall — a block that never arrives never makes the target easier. Section 5.7 explains why that distinction drives a design decision.

Why 180 s, and why only now. v2.0 left the interval open and said the proof-slot work might raise it. It did. A block that carries a client proof needs proving-time headroom inside the interval, and the decision was taken together with the larger circuit class, as one hardfork rather than two. The interval is a function of the block's own height [code]: every block the chain already holds keeps the 90 s rule it was judged by. The halflife was kept at 540 s in seconds on purpose — the mining population is concentrated, and the scenario to absorb quickly is the departure of one large miner, at the price of a somewhat noisier difficulty at steady state [code, from the rationale recorded beside the constant]. The activation block's own target is derived as half the 90-second target at that height, so the first 180 s block is not asked to be found at a 90 s difficulty [code]. On the public chain block 38 400 was found 519 s after block 38 399, and the mean interval over the first 834 blocks of the 180 s era is about 181.7 s [chain, header timestamps read on 15 October 2026].

Nothing about the 180 s interval changes the supply: halvings are heights (section 7.2). What it changes is the calendar — every "per day" figure in this document halves at block 38 400 — and the finality depth in minutes, which doubles to about 24 minutes (a local refusal to reorganise, as defined above, not a guarantee).

One known rough edge: at every start-up the node scans its own parameter sets to work out which relation generation it can serve, which is slow on small instances. Its RPC stays silent while that runs, so the node looks broken when it is not. Reducing it remains a task in section 8.

5. Proof of work

5.1 What the public network mined until block 24 845: TowerHash

TowerHash is a sponge over the Poseidon2b permutation on GF(2^128). It was the public network's proof of work from genesis to block 24 845, and it remains the seed of the current one: TowerWalk takes the TowerHash digest of the header as its input [code]. Its arithmetic is XOR and carry-less multiplication — the same algebra as the proof system. We did not invent the permutation and do not claim to; TowerHash is a construction over it: our round constants from a documented seed, our domain tags, our sponge schedule, and the nonce placed in lane 0. That last choice is worth a sentence, because it is checkable: the header absorbs 16 field elements, two per permutation, so eight permutations per attempt. With the nonce further down the schedule, everything before it is fixed across attempts and can be precomputed once as a midstate — an optimised miner would replay three permutations per attempt instead of eight. A sponge cannot be run backwards, so putting the nonce in the first absorbed pair removes that shortcut for everybody: every attempt pays for all eight permutations [code].

TowerHash is honest, cheap to verify, and good on GPUs. It is compute-bound finite-field arithmetic over a few kilobytes of state, which is the shape of work a GPU's thousands of arithmetic units exist to do. For the first 24 845 blocks a miner with a graphics card was at a substantial advantage on this network, and we did not describe that as a feature.

5.2 Why we replaced it

Not to resist ASICs, and not to decentralise mining. Section 9 says plainly that neither of those is on offer. The reason is narrower and it is the reason the whole roadmap has:

If the GPUs mine, they are not proving. Section 6 needs GPU capacity pointed at client proofs. As long as the most profitable thing a GPU could do on this network was search for nonces, the proof market competed with the chain's own security budget for the same silicon. Moving mining onto a function that a CPU runs about as well separates the two markets: proving work goes to the hardware that is good at it and gets paid for it, and mining goes to hardware that is abundant, already owned, and otherwise idle.

5.3 TowerWalk — live on the public network since block 24 846

property value

Each step reads an 8-byte cell whose address depends on the accumulator, mixes it, and writes it back. The reads are dependent: the address of step n+1 cannot be computed until step n has returned. No amount of parallelism removes that chain; only the latency of the innermost cache that holds the pad does.

The four lanes of a round are written sequentially in the reference implementation — lane 1 may read the cell lane 0 has just written. A miner may issue them concurrently and fall back to the sequential path on the rare collision, which is bit-exact and is what any serious implementation will do; it raises the number of requests in flight, and it does not shorten the chain.

512 KiB is chosen to sit inside a CPU core's private L2 cache, and outside what a GPU can give each of the thousands of concurrent threads it needs in flight to saturate its memory system.

The wall-clock spread is the machine's load, not the algorithm's variance: the cycle count moves by 3 % while the time moves by 20 %. Every rate in this document should be read with that spread attached.

The second line is the one that matters. Twelve cycles per dependent step is the load-to-use latency of that core's L2 cache: a single walk is running at the cache's floor, and no code makes one dependent chain faster. That floor bounds one chain, not the throughput of a core. A core can keep more than one walk in flight — several nonces, or two hardware threads — and overlap their memory accesses; our own open-source miner, towerminer, does exactly that and gets more out of a core than the figure above, by a margin that depends on the CPU family [measured, our own machines; not generalised here]. The 537 H/s figure is therefore a single-walk reference, not a ceiling, for anybody — including us.

One important caveat on scaling, because it also limits what we can claim. Measured on a loaded dual-socket machine (128 cores), oversubscribed with an untuned thread count, the real rate was 32 200 H/s for the whole machine [measured] — roughly half the ~68 700 H/s that multiplying 128 × 537 predicts.

That gap is a configuration effect, not a ceiling. The best thread layout depends on the CPU family — on some, one thread per physical core is best; on others, two threads per core keep more walks in flight and do better — and towerminer picks a layout per family. But we have not published a full-machine measurement in a tuned configuration, so every whole-machine and per-watt figure below is a linear extrapolation from a measured single walk on one core, and it is labelled as one.

5.5 Why this is a ceiling and not a bad kernel

The H100 result deserves an explanation, because on its own "22 554 H/s" is a number somebody will assume we failed to optimise. Two different limits are at work, and they are worth keeping apart because conflating them is easy and wrong.

Limit one: random access does not reach peak bandwidth. That card advertises 3 350 GB/s. Our kernel sustains about 755 GB/s of DRAM sector traffic — roughly 22 % of peak [derived], which is what dependent random access costs on any card. The four-lane variant, which is exact to the bit, changes nothing at saturation: both kernels land within 0.4 % of each other.

Limit two: most of that traffic is thrown away. This one is not a measurement at all, it is arithmetic.

A GPU's DRAM granularity is a 32-byte sector. One step reads 8 bytes at a random address and writes 8 back, so it moves two sectors — 64 bytes — for 16 useful ones. The ratio of useful to transferred bytes is therefore exactly 25 %, by construction and not by measurement, and the kernel sits at that ceiling because there is nowhere else to sit. Of the 755 GB/s the card moves, 189 GB/s is data the algorithm actually uses [derived].

No kernel improves that ratio. The four addresses of a round are uniformly distributed and, collisions aside, independent, so they cannot be made to share a sector; and two threads' pads are different address spaces entirely.

The other regimes were checked rather than assumed. Keeping pads resident in the GPU's large L2 trades bandwidth for latency and lands well below the DRAM regime; so does placing part of the pad in shared memory, because the duration of a step is set by its worst access and at least one of the four misses almost always [measured, on a smaller card; derived for the H100]. Combining L2-resident and DRAM-resident pads on the same card could plausibly add 15–25 % [estimate] — which moves the number, not the conclusion.

So the honest version of the claim is: on an H100-class card, nobody is going to be several times faster than this, because the limit is the memory system and not the code. That is a stronger statement than "our kernel is fast", and it is the one we are willing to defend. It is also bounded on purpose. A future card with several times the random-access throughput will produce several times the hashrate — the ratio of hashes to achieved random-access bandwidth is roughly constant — and section 9 treats that as the open risk it is rather than as an exception.

5.7 Crossing the fork

Replacing a proof-of-work digest changes the cost of a hash, and therefore the hashrate, discontinuously. Carrying the pre-fork difficulty target across the boundary would make the first post-fork block take a multiple of its intended time, on the one day the chain is being watched.

Jetsam therefore pins the target of exactly one height — the first block under the new proof of work — and lets ASERT resume from the next block on. The anchor is deliberately set on the easy side, because the two errors do not cost the same:

  • too easy — a burst of fast blocks, which ASERT tightens away within an hour;
  • too hard — the chain stops, and because ASERT anchors on the parent, a block that never arrives never makes the next target easier. Restarting requires a new binary.

The procedure was rehearsed on the test network first, and then executed on the public network at block 24 846 on 29 September 2026, 18:21 UTC. The anchor was set at exactly 2^235 [code] — chosen on the easy side, as the reasoning above requires. What happened is on the chain for anyone to read [chain]: the boundary block was found at the pinned target, the next blocks came fast (ASERT had already tightened the target four-fold within about a dozen blocks), and the chain continued with no proof errors and no reorganisation reaching back across the boundary block. The release that followed pinned the hash of block 24 846 as a hard checkpoint, so no node of that release or later can be fed an alternative history below it [code].

Two things v2.0 listed as open are therefore closed. The method itself was reused by v1.5: the activation block at 38 400 again carried a derived target — this time half the 90-second target, because the interval doubles rather than the hash cost [code] — and ASERT resumed from the next block. The target did its job; what the method does not address is a majority of the hashrate staying on the old rules after the height, which is what happened at 38 400 (section 2).

6. The proof market

Sections 1 and 4 describe a network with recursive proving capacity and a verifier already inside consensus. This section is what that is for. Since v2.0 the on-chain part of it has been written and has been live since block 38 400 (section 6.7); the catalogue it serves is empty, the SDK is not shipped, and there is no customer. Where the text below says what the project "will" do, read it as an intention with no date attached. The design is unchanged; what has moved is the line between "designed" and "in the binary", and section 6.7 draws it precisely.

6.1 The shape of the product

A client submits a computation and its committed inputs; a prover produces a proof; the proof is registered on-chain where anyone can verify it cheaply and permanently. What the client buys is not the cryptography. It is a public, durable, third-party-checkable statement that a rule was followed.

6.2 Three uses, written out

A. An agent that stayed inside its fence. A company runs a software agent that calls tools, spends money and touches customer data. It wants to be able to show a customer — or an auditor — a set of statements about a period of operation: the agent never called a tool outside its authorised list; it never spent more than the configured budget; it only ever read data belonging to the customer it was acting for; every irreversible action was preceded by the required approval; and the trace was not edited afterwards.

Each of those is a deterministic predicate over a sequence of events. Proving them is cheap — it is ordinary computation over a list, not model inference. The reason this case is first is section 1.4: an agent's actions are naturally witnessed by other parties. Signed API calls, transactions, writes into somebody else's system. The input is committed by people who have no interest in the answer. The piece that must be built well is an incremental commitment over the trace, anchored block by block, so that history cannot be rewritten after the fact.

We do not prove that the agent reasoned well. We prove it stayed inside the fence.

B. The model you were billed for is the model that ran. Customers in the inference market have no way to check that the model they were charged for is the one that served their request; silent routing to a cheaper model is a concern they raise and cannot currently settle either way. The computation is deterministic, the model is identified by a commitment to its weights, and the input is committed by the customer. What is proven is a correspondence — the responses in this billing period were produced under the commitment you were charged for — not that the model is good.

C. Attestation of a computation. A scoring decision, a prize draw, a commission calculation, a rebate allocation: cases where a party computes something that affects other people and is currently trusted to have computed it correctly. Here the rule is public, the inputs are committed in advance, and the proof replaces "trust our spreadsheet". This is the broadest family and the most boring, which is usually a good sign.

6.3 A catalogue, not a language

The client does not write a circuit. The intention — with no date — is a catalogue of ten to fifteen standard computations that a client parameterises with their own data, and an SDK that picks one, supplies the data, obtains the proof and registers it. Neither exists on the public network today.

This is not a convenience decision, it is a constraint. The recursion geometry imposes a fixed shape — the proof's dimensions and its query count are frozen in consensus. A client's relation is free, its shape is not. A catalogue is what a fixed shape allows you to promise honestly.

Identity proofs — proving an attribute of a passport or a certificate without disclosing it — have been removed from this catalogue and will not appear in it. The client form is pinned at 2^22 = 4 194 304 constraint rows [code]. RSA and ECDSA verification inside this circuit cost on the order of 10^8 to 10^9 rows [estimate] — roughly twenty-five to two hundred and fifty times that. It is not a matter of optimisation. Only computations that live natively on a binary field are viable here, and we would rather say so now than discover it in public later.

6.4 The three transaction forms

Three new transaction forms are required, and they must be frozen together in a single consensus change. Every new form means new matrices and therefore a new proof-system identity; arriving piecemeal, they would partition the network three times instead of once.

form what it does
register enrol a declaration and receive an on-chain identifier. The registry licence is paid here.
order post a bounty conditioned on a valid proof of a named statement
claim present the proof; consensus verifies it; the payment is released

The chain can already condition a payment on the revelation of a committed value, and a completed proof can be made to reveal one — so paying a prover without a timelock is implementable as-is. What is missing is the refund path if nobody proves, which needs a timelock the small proof class cannot currently afford. The two available answers are payment on delivery, and the case where the client is their own prover — which we expect to dominate.

6.5 Who pays whom

Three costs, one payer: the client.

The registry must be bounded from day one. A registered relation costs every node on the network memory for as long as it is registered — on the order of tens to hundreds of megabytes each, measured on the relation parameters the chain already carries [measured] — so a few dozen registrations is a real cost on every machine that follows the chain. The scarcity of places is not a marketing device; it is a physical constraint, and it is what justifies pricing the licence as a real cost and burning it rather than paying it to anyone. The 1 000 JTM in force today is a provisional placeholder, described in the source as symbolic (section 6.7).

Note what is not on this list: the verifier. The universal verifier is code, not a role. Miners run it, and every node re-runs it exactly as it re-runs every other consensus check. There is nobody to pay for verification and no trusted party to appoint.

6.6 Why we prove first, and why that costs nothing in security

When the first catalogue entry ships — no date is set — we intend to be the first provers. This is stated plainly because it looks like centralisation and is worth explaining rather than hiding.

A dedicated GPU pointed at an empty queue loses to any alternative use of that GPU. Two-sided markets generally start this way, and we expect the established proving networks started the same way. The point that matters is this: a centralised prover costs nothing in security. Its output is verified by consensus; it cannot produce an accepted proof of a false statement any more than a miner can produce an accepted invalid block. The only risk it carries is availability — a business risk, not a protocol risk.

The condition we will not trade away: a client must always be able to prove for themselves. Open format, and nothing in the protocol that makes us a required intermediary; a public SDK is the intended means, and it is not shipped. That is the difference between a service and a toll, and it is the first thing a serious counterparty will check. The intention is that proving opens to others on a threshold — when the queue exceeds our capacity, or when a client requires a second source — not on a date; that threshold is not defined yet.

7.4 Fees and the state-growth burn

The fee schedule sets a deterministic minimum rather than running an auction, and it has four terms [code]; a transaction may pay more than the minimum, and the excess goes to the miner [code]:

term amount (μJTM)
base 5 000
per input 100
per output 700
state growth 2 500 × multiplier × net new slots

net new slots is outputs minus inputs, floored at zero, so a transaction that consolidates — that leaves the state smaller than it found it — pays nothing on this term at all. The multiplier is a deterministic staircase on how full the state accumulator is: ×1 normally, ×2 above 50 % occupancy, ×4 above 75 %, ×8 above 90 % [code].

Every term except the last is claimable by the miner. The state-growth term is burned, and the coinbase cap counts only what is left after it, so a miner cannot recover it by any route [code].

The reason is the accumulator of section 4.3. A transaction that consumes as many slots as it creates costs the network nothing permanent. One that leaves more live slots behind has made every node carry more state, forever. The burn prices that externality at the moment it is created, and burning it rather than paying it to the miner removes any incentive to inflate the state in order to collect it.

8. Roadmap

Four stages. Each is deliverable on its own and does not block the next. Dates are deliberately absent: this project has crossed five consensus upgrades by announcing a height and a notice period, never a clock time, and the roadmap uses the same discipline. Everything below that is not marked done is an intention, not a dated commitment.

Stage 1 — the epoch-anchor upgrade. Done. Crossed on the public network at height 17 750 on 22 September 2026 [chain], after being crossed twice on the test chain and deployed as a dormant build first — the only one of the five upgrades published that way. It was the last of three upgrades the chain needed before anything new could be built on it.

Stage 2 — the CPU proof of work. Done. TowerWalk (section 5) crossed the public network at height 24 846 on 29 September 2026 [chain]. Its release was published already armed at that height, with a notice period, not as a dormant build. Of the two preconditions v2.0 listed, one was met first — the anchor was fixed against a settled difficulty (2^235 [code]). The other was not: the open-source CPU miner, towerminer, was published on 30 September 2026, the day after the fork (release date on GitHub); until then the optimised miner ran only on the maintainers' own machines, which mined a large share of the first post-fork blocks.

Stage 3 — the proof slot, the registry and the SDK. Carrier live since block 38 400 (13 October 2026); catalogue empty. v1.5.0 is the release that teaches the circuit to carry a client proof whose verification data is read from the chain's registry rather than compiled in, with the closed catalogue, the licence and the carrying fee of section 6.7 [code]. What is not shipped and has no date: the first catalogue entries (the agent-trace predicate of section 6.2 is the intended first one), the SDK, the bounty pair of transaction forms, and the opening of the prover set.

Two things about this crossing should be named plainly. First, a weakness that v1.5.0 accepted knowingly: from block 38 400 a proof covers only the blocks from 38 400 onward (section 4.2), and the v1.5.0 binary pinned no checkpoint above 24 846, so a node joining from a snapshot taken after 38 400 relied on the headers' proof of work alone for the 24 846–38 399 range. v1.5.1 closed it by pinning blocks 38 399 and 38 400 [code]. Second, a fault nobody had accepted: with about 70 % of the hashrate still on the old rules, the taller old branch captured the synchronisation of v1.5.0 nodes (section 2). v1.5.1 fixed it without changing a consensus rule: a peer whose chain a node has refused is remembered for an hour and never used as a synchronisation source; a synchronisation candidate is judged by work, not height, and must agree with the pinned blocks; and the next valid block on top of the tip is never held behind a pending synchronisation [code]. The lesson is recorded here so that the next hard fork is rehearsed against it: a branch that does not update and keeps mining, a fresh node that joins after the height, and a measure of how much of the network has actually updated before the height is reached.

Stage 3b — zero-knowledge client proofs and recursive batching. Test network only, intended. The intended v1.6 line of work would make the external proof of a client batch genuinely zero-knowledge and let the one client slot carry a recursive batch of several client proofs instead of one. It is itself a hard fork with a new relation generation and a new root (section 4.2). Only early preparatory work exists; the new relation is not written, and it has no date and no public activation height. Nothing in v1.5 should be read as "private proofs", and nothing here is a date.

Stage 4 — distribution and liquidity. Once stage 3 is demonstrable rather than described. We name no venue, no date and no price, and nothing in this document is a commitment that JTM will trade anywhere.

Parameters deliberately left open

  • The licence — its amount (1 000 JTM) and destination (burned) are marked provisional in the source [code]; the alternatives considered (a dividend to the following blocks' miners, a split, a treasury) are enumerated there and one of them would need a change inside the relation.
  • The catalogue — which computations enter, and when. Empty today.
  • The number of client slots per block — one, by construction of the carrier; raising it is a change of the carrier, not of a constant.

The two parameters v2.0 left open — the interval and the anchor — are closed (180 s from 38 400; 2^235 at 24 846).

9. What we do not claim

This section exists because the two claims a reader most expects from a CPU proof-of-work chain are the two we will not make, and because the surest way to be taken apart in public is to present the conjectured as the proven. Each subsection ends with what we do anyway.

9.1 Jetsam is not ASIC-resistant, and the gap is not small

512 KiB of SRAM is roughly 0.18–0.2 mm² on a 7 nm process [estimate, from published SRAM cell density]. The per-step logic — four lanes of add, multiply by a constant, XOR, shift — is a few thousand gates, negligible beside the memory. A hundred-square-millimetre die could hold on the order of 400 engines and produce several hundred thousand hashes per second at a few tens of watts [estimate].

Against a CPU that is roughly ×30 to ×100 per unit of capital and around ×50 per watt [estimate]. That is not a reduced advantage. That is a complete takeover of the network by whoever builds it, and there is a direct precedent: Scrypt had a comparable profile — small pad, simple per-step logic — and its first ASICs made CPU and GPU mining unprofitable within months. Algorithms that have genuinely resisted ASICs did so by requiring tens of square millimetres of logic per engine, not by having a pad.

An earlier draft of this document said the ASIC advantage would be "divided, not eliminated". That was wrong, and it is corrected here rather than quietly dropped.

What is true: the economics decide the date. An ASIC is built when the annual reward times the expected captured share exceeds the several million dollars of non-recurring engineering, plus nine to fifteen months of design time [estimate]. Below a certain network value nobody funds that. The risk is therefore correlated with the project's success, which is exactly when it hurts most.

What we do anyway: the proof of work is outside the circuit (section 3.3), so replacing it costs a consensus parameter and a digest function rather than a relation migration. That is a real defence and it is one very few proof-carrying chains have. We intend to publish, in advance, the network-value threshold at which a revision is triggered, rather than decide it under pressure on the day the first ASIC appears; that threshold is not published yet and has no date.

9.2 FPGAs are viable and we have not disproven them

A large FPGA can hold a few dozen pads in on-chip memory. Our reading of the achievable clock and step latency puts a top-end card somewhere between 8 000 and 70 000 H/s [estimate] — a range that wide because no synthesis has been run. This is our own unpublished analysis, not a citation.

At those numbers an FPGA loses badly per unit of capital, because the cards are expensive, and wins modestly per watt. It is not an economic threat. Its real significance is that it is the prototyping step for section 9.1.

9.3 A CPU proof of work attracts botnet mining

Botnet participation is documented on CPU-mined chains of any size. It is a structural cost of making mining cheap on hardware that is already everywhere, and it is the same property that makes mining accessible to honest participants.

What we do about it: nothing, and we say so. We have no mitigation to offer. Anybody who tells you they have solved this has not.

9.4 The mining population is mercenary, and rented compute is cheap

CPU miners sell their production. They do not hold, and they do not support a price.

The sharper version of the risk is about attacks, not sell pressure. In 2025 a third-party project redirected a large share of a major CPU-mined chain's hashrate into its own pool by paying miners in its own token, and reorganised blocks with it. No hashrate was rented and no marketplace was involved; the miners followed the payment, not the chain. That is the mechanism to worry about, and an unlisted algorithm does not prevent it.

Nor does an unlisted algorithm make hashrate unbuyable. General-purpose compute rents by the hour from dozens of providers. Not being listed on a hashrate marketplace raises the friction — an attacker has to build and deploy a miner rather than click a button — but it does not change the price of compute. Any claim that "0 % of this network's hashrate is rentable" is wrong, and an earlier draft of this document made it.

And the honest floor: the cost of attacking a proof-of-work chain is proportional to its total hashrate. This one is young and small. At this size the majority-attack cost is low, and it would be low under any mining algorithm. The defence is the network's size, not its choice of function, and network size is not something we can claim to have.

9.5 Implementation risk in the proof system

Jetsam runs a recursive proof system in production. Proof systems are young and soundness bugs in them are subtle. The soundness bound we quote is inherited research, not our own result, and we say so where we quote it (section 3.1).

What we do anyway: the code is public, relations are pinned by digest, each generation is selected by the block's own height, and a binary that cannot honour an armed generation refuses to start rather than forking the network quietly (section 3.4). None of that is the same as a formal proof of soundness, and we do not present it as one.

9.6 The proof market has no customer

Section 6 describes a product with a real design, a real cost model, since block 38 400 an on-chain carrier, and no identified paying client and an empty catalogue. That is the single largest open risk in this document, larger than any of the hardware questions above. It is stated here rather than in a footnote.

9.7 A client proof is not a private proof, and "post-quantum" is a narrower claim than it sounds

Two phrases that will be attached to this project by others, and that we want attached only in their exact meaning.

"Zero-knowledge." The chain does not claim zero-knowledge anywhere. The spending proof of an ordinary transaction is witness-hiding by construction, with no complete hiding proof written (section 3.1). The v1.5 client proof is not zero-knowledge — it attests that a committed computation was carried out, with public inputs and public commitments, and it is not designed to hide its witness. Private client proofs are intended v1.6 work for the test network, with no date (section 8). Amounts, addresses and fees of ordinary transactions are public, as they always were.

"Post-quantum." What is true: no elliptic curve and no signature scheme is reachable from consensus, the proof system or the proof of work, so there is nothing here for Shor's algorithm to break. What is not a theorem: that the chain is therefore secure against a quantum adversary. Security rests on Poseidon2b and on the soundness of the proof system, whose lowest documented provable figure is about 64 bits, whose certified figure against a quantum adversary is about 64.7 bits in an idealised model, and whose 128-bit figures rely on a conjecture (section 3.1). "Hash-based, no curves" is the claim we make. "Quantum-resistant" as a one-word badge is not, and we would prefer it were not made on our behalf.

9.8 The mining network is concentrated

A young CPU-mined chain has few miners, and the halflife decision of section 4.5 was taken explicitly because the departure of one large miner is the scenario to absorb. We publish no hashrate share and no miner count here; the explorer shows the coinbase of every block, and a reader can measure the concentration for themselves rather than take our word for it. Section 2 shows what concentration meant in practice at block 38 400: with most of the hashrate on the old rules, the chain went about 49 minutes without a block.

11. Governance, licensing and provenance

Who decides an upgrade. There is no on-chain governance and this document will not pretend otherwise. Consensus changes are proposed and implemented by the maintainers and published with an activation height announced in advance and a notice period. So far only one of them (v1.3) was published first as a dormant build — one that contains the new rules but does not activate them — and armed later; the others, v1.5.0 included, were published already armed. v1.5.1 (14 October 2026) changed no rule and set no height: it pinned two block ids and fixed a synchronisation fault. Node operators adopt an upgrade by choosing to run it. An operator who does not upgrade does not get a different chain until the activation height is reached; after it, the nodes that did not upgrade are on a different chain — and at block 38 400 about 70 % of the network's hashrate was (section 2).

The funds. The two development funds (section 7.3) are controlled by keys held by the maintainers. Their payouts are made by consensus on a schedule that ends at height 369 600, and each one is an on-chain transaction anyone can see. No legal entity is claimed at the time of writing; the project publishes under "the Jetsam developers", and the maintainers are pseudonymous.

Provenance. Jetsam is a derivative work of Parano1d, an Apache-2.0 codebase by another author, used under that licence. The unmodified upstream tree is kept as a visible baseline commit in the public repository, every change is a reviewable diff against it, and the full list lives in CHANGES-FROM-UPSTREAM.md with attribution in NOTICE. Jetsam is an independent network: it is not endorsed by, affiliated with or supported by the upstream project or its author, the two networks reject each other's peers and each other's work, and JTM has no relationship to the upstream's coin.

What Jetsam changed, without any comment on the upstream's own choices: the monetary policy was rewritten to halve by height under a hard 21 000 000 cap that reaches zero; the block interval is 90 s, then 180 s from height 38 400; difficulty is anchored on the parent; the proof of work is a different instantiation with different constants, tags and schedule — and, since block 24 846, a different function altogether — so the two networks' hashes are mutually invalid; the development allocation lasts two target-time years instead of three and is paid to Jetsam's own funds; and the client proof slot of v1.5 is Jetsam's own work. The recursive HistoryStep architecture and the proving stack are kept structurally as they are, because they are the good part. They are credited by name in the repository's NOTICE, together with the other upstream components the stack builds on and their own licences.

12. Glossary, references and links

Glossary

ASERT — the difficulty algorithm. Each block's target is derived from an anchor and the time elapsed since it, with an exponential response; here it is anchored on the parent's timestamp. Halflife is the time offset that doubles or halves the target.

bech32m — the address encoding. Jetsam addresses begin j1.

FRI — Fast Reed-Solomon Interactive Oracle Proof of Proximity: the low-degree test the proof system uses to make commitments checkable with a small number of queries.

GKR / sumcheck — an interactive proof technique for structured computations, used for sub-relations where it is cheaper than a generic circuit.

H/s — hashes per second: complete proof-of-work digests computed per second.

HistoryStep — the relation each block proves: that its state transition follows the rules and that the previous block's terminal commitment was valid.

IVC — incrementally verifiable computation: the general name for the technique in section 4.2, proving a long computation one step at a time with each step attesting to the last. Jetsam's HistoryStep is an instance of it.

Pack / relation generation — the compiled parameters (the "matrices") for one version of the constraint system. A block is proved and verified against the generation its own height selects, and each generation's recursion starts from its own root: genesis for the first generation, the block just below the activation height for each later one.

Midstate — the part of a hash computation that does not change between mining attempts and can therefore be computed once. Section 5.1 explains how TowerHash removes it.

Row — one constraint in the proof system, and the unit everything in the circuit is budgeted in. A block's proof has a fixed number of them available.

Slot — a seat in the state accumulator holding one live output. Spending erases the slot and returns it to the pool.

Terminal — the small commitment a block's proof exposes, meaning "everything from the root of this relation generation up to here checked out"; the next block's proof verifies it.

Finality depth — the number of blocks (8) below which a node refuses to reorganise its own chain. A local rule, not a network guarantee (section 4.5).

Checkpoint — a block hash compiled into a release; no node of that release accepts a history that does not contain it. Today: blocks 24 846, 38 399 and 38 400 [code]. Below the root of the current relation generation, checkpoints and header proof of work hold the history that the recursive proof no longer covers (section 4.2).

TowerHash — the proof-of-work function of the public network's first 24 845 blocks: a sponge over Poseidon2b. Still computed as the seed of TowerWalk.

TowerWalk — the cache-resident proof-of-work function of section 5, live on the public network since block 24 846.

Catalogue — the closed list of client computations (identified by the digest of their matrices) that a release allows to be registered. Empty on the public network at v1.5.1.

Client slot — the place in a v1.5 block for at most one client proof.

Activation height — the block height at which a set of consensus rules takes effect. Always announced in blocks; a calendar date for it is an estimate.
